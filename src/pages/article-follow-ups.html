Follow-ups · 3 min read

A follow-up worth replying to.

Keep a trip conversation moving with useful context, clear ownership and a next step that respects the traveller.

In this story

  1. Start with the open question.
  2. Make the next reply easy.
  3. Agree on timing and ownership.
  4. Be precise about changing details.
  5. Know when to leave the door open.

Start with the open question.

A traveller who has not replied is not always a lost enquiry. They may be comparing dates, waiting for a family decision or trying to understand what is included. Before sending another message, read the conversation and identify the decision that is still open. A useful follow-up helps with that decision rather than simply reminding someone that you are waiting.

If the last conversation was about choosing between two hotels, lead with the difference that matters: location, room arrangement or travel time. Avoid introducing an entirely new destination unless the traveller has asked for alternatives. Keep the message connected to the trip they are already considering.

Make the next reply easy.

An open-ended “Any update?” places the work back on the traveller. Offer a small, answerable question instead. For example: “Would you prefer the quieter hotel near the beach, or the central option with shorter transfers? I can update the proposal around either one.” The reply can be brief while still helping your team move forward.

Use one main question per message. If you need several details, explain why they matter and group them clearly. A short list of remaining decisions is easier to answer than a paragraph that mixes dates, rooms, flights and payment requests.

Agree on timing and ownership.

When a traveller says they will discuss the trip over the weekend, note that context and agree on a suitable time to reconnect. That is more useful than applying the same daily reminder to every enquiry. Record a follow-up date and a responsible teammate so the conversation does not depend on somebody remembering it.

Before contacting someone, check whether another colleague has already replied. Keep a short note of the message and the next action. One clear owner can coordinate the enquiry while other teammates contribute without sending competing requests.

Be precise about changing details.

Availability and quoted arrangements can change. If a supplier has given you a specific validity period, communicate it accurately and keep the original context with the enquiry. Avoid artificial countdowns or a claim that a room is “almost gone” when you have no confirmation.

When an older proposal needs checking, say so plainly: “Once you confirm the dates, I will recheck the hotel and transfer arrangements.” This sets a useful expectation without presenting an earlier quotation as a current guarantee. If something changes, explain the difference before asking the traveller to decide.

Know when to leave the door open.

After a reasonable sequence of helpful messages, give the traveller room to pause. A simple closing note can explain that you are setting the enquiry aside for now and that they can return when the dates or plans are clearer. Keep the record available so a future conversation has continuity.

Review your follow-ups as a team. Look for unanswered questions, duplicate messages and proposals with no next action. The aim is a more useful conversation, not a higher message count. Start with a handful of active enquiries and improve the next message each traveller receives.

Give the traveller a reason to reply, and a simple way to do it.
