A better trip begins with a clearer brief.

Ask the questions that shape a useful itinerary before your team starts comparing hotels and building proposals.

In this story

  2. Understand the people taking the trip.
  3. Turn a budget into a shared understanding.
  4. Ask what a good day feels like.
  5. Read the brief back before building.

Separate the essentials from preferences.

A destination and a date are a beginning, not a complete trip brief. Before researching options, establish the group size, approximate budget, departure point and length of stay. Then ask which details are fixed and which can move. A flexible departure day may open different options; a family event on a specific evening may set the whole itinerary.

Label these details clearly. “Must arrive before Friday afternoon” means something different from “would prefer a morning arrival.” When your team can see the difference, they can make sensible trade-offs without repeatedly returning to the traveller for the same clarification.

Understand the people taking the trip.

Ask about the travelling group in practical terms. Room sharing, preferred pace and the amount of walking people enjoy can shape a proposal more than a long list of attractions. Invite the traveller to share access or comfort requirements they want considered, without making assumptions based on age or family structure.

Only collect personal information needed for the current planning stage. A discovery conversation does not require a folder full of identity documents. Explain why a detail matters, record it carefully and avoid spreading sensitive information through unnecessary screenshots or informal group chats.

Turn a budget into a shared understanding.

A budget is useful only when both sides understand what it covers. Ask whether the figure is for the whole group or per traveller, and whether flights, accommodation, transfers, meals and activities need to fit within it. Use a clear written summary to confirm the scope before your team builds an option.

If the requested experience and available budget do not appear to match, explain the choices early. You could adjust the travel dates, reduce the number of stops or compare different hotel arrangements. Present these as options for discussion rather than quietly removing important elements from the proposal.

Ask what a good day feels like.

A traveller may say “relaxed holiday” and still send a list of eight attractions. Ask them to describe one ideal day. Do they want a slow breakfast and time at the hotel, or an early start followed by a full day outside? The answer helps translate a broad preference into a realistic daily plan.

Keep travel time visible when discussing the route. A map can make two places look close while transfers take a meaningful part of the day. Explain where a busy schedule may feel rushed, and let the traveller choose what deserves more time.

Read the brief back before building.

End discovery with a short summary: who is travelling, what is fixed, what they want to spend and the experience they care about most. Add any questions still awaiting an answer. Invite corrections before you begin detailed supplier work; a two-minute clarification can prevent a proposal built around the wrong assumption.

Keep the agreed brief with the enquiry and update it when decisions change. A teammate should be able to understand the current plan without replaying every message. That shared starting point makes research, handovers and proposal review much easier to coordinate.

A good brief explains what matters, not just where someone wants to go.
