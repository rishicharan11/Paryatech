Remember the details. Welcome them back.

Use helpful context to make a returning traveller’s next conversation feel considered.

In this story

  2. Keep notes specific and respectful.
  3. Check what has changed.
  4. Make continuity a team habit.
  5. Follow up with a reason.

Begin with what was useful last time.

A traveller returns after a family holiday and asks about a short break. Their previous conversation may hold useful context: the pace they enjoyed, a hotel style they preferred or a transfer arrangement that worked well. Review those details before asking them to repeat the same background.

Use that context as a starting point for a question. “Last time you preferred a quieter base; would that suit this trip too?” is more helpful than assuming the next journey should follow the same pattern. The destination, companions, purpose and budget may all have changed since the previous booking.

Keep notes specific and respectful.

Useful notes describe an expressed preference or an agreed arrangement. “Asked for fewer hotel changes on the last trip” gives a teammate something concrete to discuss. Vague labels such as “difficult customer” do not explain what happened and can distort the next interaction before it begins.

Keep only information that serves a clear purpose in helping with the traveller’s enquiries. Follow your agency’s established practices for collecting, accessing and retaining personal information. Put a preference in context where possible, so a request made for one journey is not treated as a permanent description of the person.

Check what has changed.

Ask a few focused questions before building the next proposal. Who is travelling this time? How much flexibility is there on dates? Is the trip for rest, exploration or a particular occasion? A returning traveller should still have room to tell you what matters now.

If someone previously travelled with young children, do not assume the same group is joining every future trip. Similarly, a past spending range is a discussion point rather than a current instruction. Updating the working brief early helps the team avoid preparing a polished proposal around a preference that no longer applies.

Make continuity a team habit.

A relationship should not depend entirely on the one colleague who handled the last trip. Before handing a returning enquiry to someone else, share a brief, relevant summary: previous journey, useful preferences, current request and open questions. Make clear which details the traveller has reconfirmed.

Keep the summary tied to the active conversation. If a traveller corrects a preference, make the update visible wherever your team normally records the brief. This helps prevent another colleague from repeating the old assumption. The aim is a considerate handover, with enough context to continue naturally.

Follow up with a reason.

When you contact a past traveller, offer something relevant and explain why you thought of them. A useful follow-up might revisit an idea they asked you to explore or check whether they want to continue an unfinished enquiry. Use the communication preferences they have agreed with your agency.

After a completed trip, invite practical feedback without assuming that every detail was perfect. Ask what worked and what they would change next time. Record the actionable points in the appropriate place. The value of remembering a traveller lies in making the next conversation more thoughtful, while still giving them the freedom to want something different.

Good context opens a better conversation. It does not replace one.
