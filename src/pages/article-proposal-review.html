Before you send the proposal.

A focused review of dates, inclusions and decisions can make the next version clearer for everyone reading it.

In this story

  1. Check it against the agreed brief.
  2. Read the route as a sequence.
  3. Make the amount understandable.
  4. Leave a clear next decision.
  5. Send one current version.

Check it against the agreed brief.

Before checking the presentation, check the trip. Compare the proposal with the traveller’s latest agreed dates, group size, room arrangement and priorities. Make sure a recent change has reached every relevant part of the document. An attractive itinerary can still be difficult to trust if the summary says five nights and the daily plan contains four.

Look particularly closely at details carried over from an earlier option. Destination names, departure points and traveller numbers can survive a copy-and-edit workflow. A short comparison with the brief gives the review a reliable starting point instead of depending on how familiar the proposal feels.

Read the route as a sequence.

Follow the itinerary from arrival to departure. Check where travellers are staying each night, which transfers connect the stops and how much of the day is spent moving. Make sure the proposal distinguishes a suggested activity from an arrangement included in the quotation.

Consider the experience as well as the list of places. If a day includes an early arrival, a transfer and a busy afternoon, explain the pace honestly. Where details remain tentative, say what is awaiting confirmation. The traveller should be able to picture the trip without guessing how the separate parts connect.

Make the amount understandable.

Check the total and the basis on which it is presented. State whether the amount applies to the group or to each traveller, and keep the currency visible. Where a figure is based on a specific number of people or room arrangement, present that context close to the amount.

Review the inclusions and exclusions together. A traveller should not have to compare three different messages to understand what the quotation covers. Use the actual terms supplied for the arrangements being proposed, and have the responsible person check any payment or booking conditions before the document is sent.

Leave a clear next decision.

A proposal can be informative and still leave the traveller unsure what to do. End with a useful next step: choose between two hotel options, confirm dates for an availability check, or arrange a call to discuss the route. Keep the request specific enough that the traveller can respond without composing a new brief.

If several choices are open, indicate which one matters first. You might need the departure date before you can compare a flight option, for example. Explain that dependency in ordinary language. This helps the conversation progress in a sensible order rather than collecting disconnected answers.

Send one current version.

Give the proposal a recognisable title or version label and record which version you shared. When making a revision, briefly explain what changed. A traveller comparing hotel options should not also have to work out whether the transfer or trip length has changed in the background.

Ask a teammate to review unfamiliar or complex arrangements when appropriate. Keep the review focused: agreement with the brief, a coherent route, readable amounts and a clear next action. Then store the sent version with the enquiry so the next conversation begins from the same information on both sides.

Review the proposal as the traveller will read it: one decision at a time.
