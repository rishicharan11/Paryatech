A calmer rhythm for a busy travel team.

Build a small daily review around ownership, waiting decisions and the handovers that keep enquiries moving.

In this story

  1. Begin with the work that needs attention.
  2. Give each enquiry a coordinating owner.
  3. Separate doing from waiting.
  4. Reserve time to finish something.
  5. End with a useful record.

Begin with the work that needs attention.

A busy agency can have plenty of activity and still miss an important next step. Rather than reviewing every message at the start of the day, look for enquiries that need attention: a promised proposal, a traveller waiting for an answer, a supplier clarification or a colleague who needs a decision to continue.

Keep the review focused on exceptions and commitments. Each person should be able to explain what they are moving forward and where they need help. This makes the conversation useful for a small team without turning a quick check-in into a full reading of the enquiry list.

Give each enquiry a coordinating owner.

Several people may contribute to a trip, but one person should coordinate the next action. That owner can see whether a reply has been sent, who is checking the arrangement and what the traveller has been promised. Ownership is about continuity rather than doing every task personally.

When work changes hands, make the change explicit. A short handover should include the current trip brief, the last meaningful decision and the next commitment. If somebody is away, agree who is covering their active conversations before another teammate discovers an unanswered request by chance.

Separate doing from waiting.

An enquiry awaiting a traveller’s decision needs different attention from a proposal your team has not started. Make that distinction visible. For each waiting item, note who you are waiting for and when it would be useful to check again. For each task your team owns, record the next concrete action.

Try replacing vague notes such as “follow up” with a specific instruction: “Ask whether the family prefers the two-room option before requesting a revised quotation.” The action becomes easier to complete and a covering colleague can understand the reason behind it.

Reserve time to finish something.

Messages arrive throughout the day, while research and proposal writing need uninterrupted attention. Agree how the team will cover incoming conversations while a colleague completes a focused task. Even a modest protected window can make it easier to finish a proposal without repeatedly rebuilding the same train of thought.

Use a clear way to flag something that genuinely needs an immediate decision. If every message is treated as urgent, the team loses the ability to distinguish a time-sensitive issue from an ordinary update. Discuss the difference together using examples from your own workflow.

End with a useful record.

Before the day closes, check tomorrow’s commitments and any conversations another teammate may need to cover. Update the enquiry record while the details are fresh. A short, accurate note about a traveller’s decision is more useful than a long summary written several days later.

Review the rhythm after a week of using it. Ask which questions repeated, where handovers still needed explanation and whether any enquiries lost a clear owner. Adjust one habit at a time. The goal is a shared picture of the work, with enough structure to support the people doing it.

A useful team review ends with clear actions, not a longer meeting.
