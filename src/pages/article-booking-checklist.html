A booking checklist everyone can follow.

Turn a traveller’s yes into a clear sequence of checks, confirmations and handovers.

In this story

  1. Define what the traveller has approved.
  2. Give each check an owner.
  3. Compare the details across records.
  4. Make the handover useful.
  5. Close the loop with the traveller.

Define what the traveller has approved.

A traveller replies, “Let’s go ahead,” after a conversation containing two hotel options and a revised departure date. Before treating the plan as settled, confirm exactly which version they mean. Summarise the selected dates, party size, room arrangement and agreed package in one clear message.

Give the approved version a recognisable reference in your working record. If there is still an unanswered question, keep it visible rather than folding it into an assumption. A booking checklist starts with a shared understanding of what the agency has been asked to arrange and what still needs a decision.

Give each check an owner.

Write the checklist as actions someone can complete. “Check transfer availability with the supplier” tells a teammate more than “transfers.” Add an owner and a useful target time where timing matters. If two people depend on the same supplier reply, identify who will request it and who needs the answer.

Keep completion separate from progress. A request sent is not an arrangement confirmed. Simple states such as awaiting reply, needs review and confirmed can make that difference readable. Use the terms your team already understands and agree what evidence is needed before an item can move to confirmed.

Compare the details across records.

Check the traveller-facing summary against the latest supplier information. Look carefully at travel dates, overnight locations, passenger names, room types and transfer endpoints. A short mismatch can be hard to spot when each document is viewed separately, so compare related details together.

For example, a changed arrival date may affect the first hotel night and the airport pickup. Follow the change through the whole trip instead of correcting only the itinerary. Ask for clarification through your normal booking process whenever two records disagree. Keep sensitive traveller information within the access and handling practices agreed by your agency.

Make the handover useful.

When operations takes over from sales, include the agreed plan, outstanding actions and any promises already made to the traveller. Link to the relevant working records rather than forwarding a long message history without explanation. A colleague should be able to identify the next task without reconstructing the whole sale.

Explain unusual arrangements in plain language. If a traveller requested adjacent rooms and the supplier has only acknowledged the request, preserve that distinction. Do not let a preference become a guarantee during the handover. Ask the receiving colleague to flag anything they cannot confidently act on.

Close the loop with the traveller.

Once you have checked the confirmed details, send a concise update explaining the current status and what happens next. If something remains pending, name it and set a realistic expectation for the next update. Keep your communication aligned with what your records actually support.

After completing the checklist, note any recurring source of confusion. Perhaps revised hotel choices keep getting mixed with approved ones, or transfer details arrive in an inconsistent format. Improve that part of the process for the next booking. A checklist stays useful when it reflects real handovers and real questions from your team.

A confirmed next step is more useful than an assumed one.
