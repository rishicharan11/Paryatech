Make room for your busiest season.

Prepare ownership, handovers and working information before the enquiry queue grows.

In this story

  1. Walk through one real enquiry.
  2. Agree who watches the queue.
  3. Prepare the information you reuse.
  4. Create a simple escalation route.
  5. Review the work while it is happening.

Walk through one real enquiry.

Before a busy period, choose a recent enquiry and follow it from the first message to the latest action. Look at where the brief was captured, who contacted suppliers and how the next colleague found the context. This gives the team a concrete process to discuss instead of a general request to “be more organised.”

Identify the moments that required someone to search for information or ask a colleague what happened. Pick one or two improvements you can actually put into practice. A clearer handover note or a visible next action may be more useful than introducing several unfamiliar routines at once.

Agree who watches the queue.

Decide how new enquiries will be reviewed and assigned during the working day. Make the arrangement visible to the people involved, including what happens when an owner is away. A shared inbox still needs a shared understanding of who is expected to act.

Talk through common edge cases. Who responds when an enquiry arrives near the end of a shift? Who covers a colleague who is already dealing with a complex booking? Avoid promising response times your staffing cannot support. Give the team a clear way to raise a request that needs attention sooner.

Prepare the information you reuse.

Review the working material your team repeatedly sends or checks: briefing questions, proposal introductions, supplier contact details and handover checklists. Remove superseded versions from the places people normally search. Mark anything that needs verification before it can be reused in a traveller-facing conversation.

Keep reusable wording easy to adapt. A template can prompt someone to confirm dates and group size, but it should not fill in unknown answers. Ask a teammate to use the material for a sample enquiry. If they need extra explanation to complete it, improve the prompts while there is still time.

Create a simple escalation route.

Write down where a colleague should go when an issue falls outside their authority or needs another person’s help. Use practical examples from your own process: a supplier has not replied, a proposal needs a second review, or a traveller has requested a substantial change.

An escalation note should include the situation, what has already been checked and the decision needed. Agree who receives it and how the sender will know it has been picked up. This reduces the chance that a difficult task sits between two people, with each expecting the other to move it forward.

Review the work while it is happening.

Set a manageable rhythm for checking unassigned enquiries, unanswered supplier requests and traveller decisions that are still pending. Keep the review focused on the next action and the person responsible. If everything is marked urgent, discuss what genuinely needs attention first.

At the end of a busy stretch, ask the team which change helped and where the process still became confusing. Keep the improvements that made daily work clearer. Preparation is not finished when a checklist is written; it becomes useful when the team can follow it on a crowded day without needing another meeting to explain it.
